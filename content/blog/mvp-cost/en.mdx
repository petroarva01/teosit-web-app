"How much does an app cost?" is a bit like asking how much a house costs. The honest answer is "it depends" — but that's not useful when you're planning a budget. So instead of a single number, here's how the cost of an MVP is actually built, where the money goes, and which decisions move it the most.

## First, what an MVP is (and isn't)

A minimum viable product is the smallest version of your product that real users can use to solve a real problem — small enough to build quickly, complete enough that people would actually use it.

It's **not** a clickable prototype, and it's **not** version 1.0 with everything on the wishlist. The most common reason MVP budgets explode is that the "minimum" quietly grows: an admin panel, three user roles, a chat, social login with four providers, a dashboard with charts. Each item seems small. Together they double the project.

A useful test for every feature: _if we launched without this, would users still get the core value?_ If yes, it goes on the list for later.

## The formula behind every quote

Under every estimate there's the same simple formula:

**cost = scope × complexity × team rate**

You control the first two much more than the third. A cheaper team rarely makes a project cheaper if the scope is unclear — it usually just makes it longer. Clear scope is where the real savings are.

## Where the money goes

Here's a rough guide to how a typical mobile MVP budget splits between phases:

- **Discovery and planning — around 5–10%.** Turning the idea into a list of screens and features, agreeing on what's in and out, choosing the tech. This is the cheapest phase and the one that saves the most money later.
- **UI/UX design — around 10–15%.** User flows, wireframes, the visual design of every screen, and a clickable prototype to test before code is written.
- **Development — around 55–65%.** The app itself, the backend and database, the admin tools, integrations like payments and notifications.
- **Testing and fixes — around 10–15%.** On real devices, both platforms, different screen sizes, slow networks, edge cases.
- **Launch — around 5%.** Store listings, screenshots, privacy forms, review, production setup and monitoring.

If a quote has almost nothing for discovery, design or testing, the money hasn't disappeared — it will be spent later, usually on rework.

## What makes an MVP more expensive

Some features cost far more than they look. These are the ones we ask about first:

- **Accounts and roles.** Sign-up, login, password reset and account deletion are a small project on their own. Several user types (customer, provider, admin) multiply screens and permissions.
- **Payments.** Subscriptions or in-app purchases, refunds, invoices, store rules for digital goods. Payments always need extra care and testing.
- **Real-time features.** Chat, live tracking, collaborative editing. They need a different kind of backend and are hard to test.
- **Integrations.** Every third-party system — a CRM, a booking provider, a bank API — adds work, and its documentation is rarely as good as it promises.
- **Admin panel.** Someone has to manage users and content. Often it can start as a simple internal tool instead of a polished product.
- **Offline support and sync.** Making data work without a connection and merge correctly later is real engineering.
- **Many languages.** Every language adds translation, testing and store listings.
- **Unclear requirements.** The most expensive item of all. Changing direction mid-build costs more than any feature.

## What makes it cheaper

- **One codebase for iOS and Android.** With React Native, both apps come from the same code — one team, one set of features.
- **Managed backend services.** Tools like Supabase or Firebase give you a database, authentication and file storage out of the box, instead of building them from scratch.
- **Ready-made components where they're good enough.** Not every screen needs a custom design. Spend design effort where users feel it.
- **One platform first, if it fits.** If all your early users are on one platform or on the web, launching there first can be a good trade-off.
- **A ruthless feature list.** Every "later" feature is money you keep until you know users want it.

## The costs after launch

The build isn't the whole budget. Plan for these too:

- **Developer accounts.** Apple charges $99 per year; Google Play has a one-time $25 fee.
- **Hosting and services.** Database, file storage, email, maps, analytics, error tracking. Many start free and grow with your users.
- **Maintenance.** iOS and Android release new versions every year, libraries update, and stores change their rules. Budget for regular updates — a common rule of thumb is around 15–20% of the build cost per year.
- **Improvements.** Once real users arrive, you'll learn what to build next. That's the point of an MVP, so leave room for it.

## Fixed price or paying by time?

There are two common ways to pay for an MVP:

- **Fixed price** works when the scope is clear and written down. You know the total upfront, and changes are handled as separate, agreed additions.
- **Time and materials** (a dedicated team or hourly) works when the product is still taking shape. You pay for the work done, and you can change direction freely.

Many projects use both: a fixed-price discovery and design phase that produces a clear scope, then a fixed-price or team-based build.

## Red flags in a quote

- A single number with no breakdown of features or phases.
- No time for design, testing or launch.
- "Unlimited revisions" or a promise that everything is included.
- No mention of who owns the code, the designs and the store accounts. (The answer should be: you, from day one.)
- A price far below others, with no explanation of what's different.

## How to get a quote you can trust

Before you ask for a price, write down:

1. **Who the users are** and the one problem the app solves for them.
2. **The three to five things a user must be able to do** in the first version.
3. **Platforms:** iOS, Android, web, or a mix.
4. **Anything that touches money, other systems or sensitive data.**
5. **Your timeline and the budget range you're comfortable with.**
